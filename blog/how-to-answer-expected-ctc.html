How to Answer "What Is Your Expected CTC?" (Scripts for Indian Developers)

Updated 16 October 2026 · FY 2026-27 rules

"What's your expected CTC?" usually comes up in the first recruiter call, sometimes before you know the level, the team, or the salary band. Answer too low and you've capped your offer. Answer too high without context and you may get filtered out. Here's a practical way to prepare a number, when to share it, and exactly what to say.

Why recruiters ask so early

It's mostly a budget check. The recruiter has a band for the role and wants to know if you're likely to fit before investing interview time. That's reasonable, but the first number either side says tends to anchor the rest of the conversation. Your goal is to learn their band first if you can. If you can't, give a well-prepared number that leaves room above your real target.

Before the call: build your three numbers

  1. Walk-away number. The fixed pay below which you'd stay where you are. Include the cost of switching: a notice buyout, a lost bonus, relocation.
  2. Target. What you'd be genuinely happy to sign. Base it on what this level pays at this kind of company. Ask peers who interviewed recently, ask the recruiter about the level, and look at offer-sharing discussions in developer communities. Treat anonymous numbers as rough signals, not facts.
  3. Anchor. The number you say out loud. Usually about 10–15% above your target, so that after negotiation you land near the target.

Then convert each to monthly in-hand with the CTC to in-hand calculator. A "₹24 LPA" figure means very different things depending on how much is variable, PF and gratuity. Knowing the monthly number keeps you from accepting a CTC that looks bigger but pays less.

Rule 1: Talk in fixed pay, not total CTC

Total CTC can include variable pay, joining bonuses, ESOPs, insurance and gratuity. If you say "₹24 lakh" and the offer comes back as ₹24 lakh CTC with ₹3 lakh variable and a one-time joining bonus, your fixed pay is far below what you meant. Say "fixed" explicitly every time.

Rule 2: Try to get their range first

Thanks for asking. I'd like to understand the role and level first, so we're comparing the right numbers. Could you share the budgeted range for this position? If it's in the right ballpark, I'm happy to go ahead.

Many recruiters will share a range if you ask politely. If the top of their range is below your walk-away number, you've saved everyone weeks.

Rule 3: If they push, give one confident number

Based on the role and the current market for [backend/full-stack] engineers at [X] years' experience, I'm looking at roughly ₹[anchor] LPA fixed, though I'm flexible depending on the overall structure.

Why one number and not a range? If you say "₹22–26 lakh", most companies hear "₹22 lakh". If you must give a range, make the bottom of it your target, not your walk-away.

When the application form demands a number

Some portals won't let you submit without an "expected CTC" field. Enter your anchor as a fixed-pay figure. If there's a notes field, add: "Expected fixed; open to discussing overall structure based on the role." Don't enter 0 or 1 to dodge it. It can look careless and may break the recruiter's filter.

The current CTC question

Expected and current CTC usually come together, and in India it's common for companies to frame offers as a hike on current pay. You can shift the frame without being evasive:

My current fixed is ₹[X] LPA. Since this is a step up in scope, I'm evaluating offers based on the new role rather than a percentage hike on my current pay.

Never inflate your current CTC. Many companies ask for payslips, Form 16 or bank statements during the offer or background verification stage. A mismatch can cost you the offer, which isn't worth any hike.

If they say "We can only offer X% hike"

Some companies cap hikes as a percentage of current pay. Bring the conversation back to the role and the market:

I understand there's a guideline. My expectation is based on the scope of this role and what the market pays at this level, not on my current salary, which reflects a smaller role. Is there flexibility if the interview panel sees me at the higher level?

If the fixed pay truly can't move, ask about a joining bonus, an earlier performance review or additional ESOPs. It's often easier for a company to make a one-time payment than to change the band.

If you hold a competing offer

Use it honestly and specifically:

I want to be transparent: I have an offer from [Company] at ₹[X] fixed. You're my first choice because of [specific reason]. If you can match ₹[X] fixed, I'd happily close with you.

Never invent an offer. Recruiters often ask to see the letter, and the Indian tech hiring world is smaller than it looks.

A worked example

Say you're on ₹14 lakh fixed with three years' experience, interviewing for an SDE-2 role. From conversations with peers and the recruiter's hints about the level, you decide:

  • Walk-away: ₹19 lakh fixed (below that, the switch plus a notice buyout isn't worth it)
  • Target: ₹22 lakh fixed
  • Anchor: ₹25 lakh fixed

In the first call you ask for their range. They say "₹20–24 lakh". You respond: "Thanks, that helps. Given the scope, I'd be looking at the upper end, around ₹24–25 lakh fixed." If the offer arrives at ₹21 lakh, you counter with one clear number (₹23.5 lakh) and a commitment to sign quickly if they get there. These figures are illustrative; plug your own into the calculator to see what each means monthly.

Mistakes to avoid

  • Quoting monthly in-hand ("I want ₹1.5 lakh a month"). Recruiters work in annual CTC, and the conversion is ambiguous. Use annual fixed.
  • Giving a number before knowing the level. An SDE-2 and SDE-3 band can be far apart. Ask which level you're being considered for.
  • Apologising for your number. State it calmly and stop talking.
  • Accepting on the call. "Thank you, I'll review the breakup and get back to you within 24–48 hours" is always fine.
  • Leaving changes verbal. Get every revision in an updated offer letter.
