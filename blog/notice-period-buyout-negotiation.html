How to Negotiate a Notice Period Buyout (With Scripts for Indian Developers)

Updated 16 October 2026 · FY 2026-27 rules

You've cleared the interviews, the offer is ready, and the recruiter asks the dreaded question: "Can you join in 30 days?" Your appointment letter says 90. A notice period buyout, where you (or your new employer) pay your current employer for the days you don't serve, is often the way through. Here's how to work out what it costs, who should pay, and what to say, with scripts you can adapt.

First, read your own documents. Whether you can buy out notice at all depends on your appointment letter and your company's HR policy. In many companies it's at the employer's discretion, not your right. Everything below assumes your policy allows a buyout or early release, or that you're asking for one.

Step 1: Find the exact rules that apply to you

Before talking to anyone, look in your appointment letter, HR policy portal or employee handbook for:

  • Notice length, and whether it differs during probation.
  • Buyout formula. Is recovery based on basic salary or gross salary for the unserved days? This one detail can double the amount.
  • Leave adjustment. Some companies let you offset earned-leave balance against notice; others don't allow leave during notice at all.
  • Who approves early release. Usually your manager, then HR.

If the policy is unclear, ask HR in writing: "If I'm released on [date], how will the recovery for the remaining notice days be calculated?" A written answer avoids surprises in your full-and-final settlement.

Step 2: Calculate the buyout amount

A common formula is: monthly salary component ÷ 30 (or the days in that month) × unserved days. Check which divisor your company uses.

Example: your notice is 90 days, the new company wants you in 30, so 60 days are unserved.

Same person, same notice, very different numbers. Know which one is yours before you ask the new company for money.

In practice, the recovery is often deducted from your full-and-final settlement (unpaid salary, leave encashment). If those dues don't cover it, you pay the difference. That timing matters for the next step.

Step 3: Try for early release first

A buyout isn't the only route. If you've been easy to work with and leave a solid handover, many managers will agree to release you early, sometimes without recovery. Go in with a plan, not just a request.

Hi [Manager], I've accepted an offer and the new team needs me to start by [date]. I know my notice is [90] days, so I've drafted a handover plan: [owner for each module/service], documentation for [X], and I'll finish [critical task] before I go. Would you support an early release on [date]? I'll make sure nothing is left hanging.

Ask for a partial release too. Even serving 45 days instead of 90 halves your buyout.

Step 4: Ask the new employer to cover it

Companies that want you sooner often have a way to pay: a joining bonus, a buyout reimbursement, or both. The right time to ask is after they've decided they want you (a verbal or written offer) but before you've signed.

My notice period is [90] days. To join by [date], I'd need to buy out about [60] days, which costs roughly ₹[amount] under my company's policy. Could the company cover this through a joining bonus or buyout reimbursement? If we can sort this out, I can confirm the [date] start.

Why this works: it's specific, it ties the money to their preferred start date, and it ends with a commitment.

Ask for the after-tax amount

A joining bonus is taxed as salary. If you need ₹1,60,000 in hand to pay a buyout, a ₹1,60,000 bonus won't cover it. A rough gross-up is: amount needed ÷ (1 − your marginal tax rate including cess).

A direct reimbursement against your former employer's recovery letter may be treated differently from a bonus, so ask the new company how they process it and check with a CA if the amount is large.

Step 5: Get it in writing, and read the clawback clause

  • Revised offer letter. The joining bonus or reimbursement should appear in the offer, not just in a recruiter's WhatsApp message.
  • Payment timing. You usually pay your old employer at exit, but the bonus may come with your first or second salary. Ask when it's paid and plan for the cash gap.
  • Clawback. Joining bonuses commonly come with a clause to repay all or part of it if you leave within a set period, often 12 months. Know the terms before you sign.
  • Joining date. Confirm the date in writing once your release date is approved.

Step 6: Close out properly with your current employer

Whatever you negotiate, leave with a relieving letter and an experience letter. Background verification at the new company usually checks your previous employment, and some employers ask for the relieving letter before onboarding. Leaving without completing exit formalities (absconding) can create problems in BGV and with your full-and-final settlement. A buyout saves you time; skipping the process doesn't.

Common mistakes

  • Promising a date before release is approved. Say "I'm targeting [date], pending release" until it's confirmed.
  • Quoting a buyout number you haven't verified. If the new company reimburses ₹1.2 lakh and your recovery turns out to be ₹2.8 lakh, the gap is yours.
  • Asking for the bonus after signing. Your leverage is highest before you accept.
  • Forgetting tax. Ask for the grossed-up amount, or ask for reimbursement against actuals.

Quick checklist

  1. Read the notice and buyout clauses; confirm basic vs gross in writing.
  2. Calculate unserved days and the buyout amount.
  3. Ask your manager for early or partial release, with a handover plan.
  4. Ask the new employer to cover the remainder, grossed up for tax.
  5. Get the bonus, timing and clawback terms in a revised offer letter.
  6. Leave with a relieving letter and experience letter.
